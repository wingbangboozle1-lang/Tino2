Why a straight line is the wrong planning tool

What the record showsEvidenceWhy it changes a decisionSource
Inflation is not a constantOver the 35 complete years from 1991 to 2025, annual CPI-U change averaged 2.62%, ranged from −0.36% to 8.00%, and exceeded 3% in 10 of those years.A single 3% cost assumption is reasonable in the middle and badly wrong at both ends. Costs that compound quietly for 24 years magnify that error.BLS CPI-U via FRED · CPIAUCNS ↗
Two recent years broke the patternCPI-U rose 4.70% in 2021, 8.00% in 2022 and 4.12% in 2023 as annual averages, after a decade mostly under 2%.A planner who calibrates on 2010–2020 understates cost growth; one who calibrates on 2021–2023 overstates it. Both are extrapolation errors.BLS CPI-U via FRED · CPIAUCNS ↗
The tax base is capped, prices are notProposition 13 limits the annual adjustment to a property’s factored base year value to 2%. Across the 51 published assessment years from 1976-77 to 2026-27 the factor hit the full 2% cap in 40 years, was lower 11 times, and compounded at about 1.81% a year.The assessed base and the market price diverge over time by design. Modeling them with the same growth rate is a structural error, not a rounding error.BOE · 2026-27 inflation factor (LTA 2026/002) ↗
Official projections stop long before the horizonCBO’s February 2026 baseline runs 2026 to 2036 and projects PCE inflation returning to roughly 2% around 2030.A 2045 or 2050 transfer sits far outside any official projection. Anything shown for those years is a scenario, whoever produces it.Congressional Budget Office ↗
Regional hazard is priced separatelyUSGS estimated a 72% chance of at least one magnitude 6.7 or greater earthquake somewhere in the Bay region between 2014 and 2043.Standard homeowners policies generally exclude earthquake damage, so an uninsured loss is a genuine balance-sheet event rather than a premium line.USGS · Bay Area outlook ↗
Borrowing is not cheapFreddie Mac’s survey averaged 7.11% for a 30-year fixed rate as of 12 November 2026.Any plan that assumes borrowing against a property to fund a repair or a purchase has to clear that hurdle. No borrowing is modeled here.Freddie Mac · mortgage rates ↗

Two rates that move differently

QuantityLong-run observed behaviorOfficial basisSource
Market price of a homeNot published as a national constant. Regional indexes exist; a single parcel is not an index.FHFA publishes house-price indexes; they are not a forecast for one house.FHFA · house price index via FRED ↗
Assessed value used for taxCapped at 2% a year absent a reassessment event, and the assessor enrolls the lower of the factored base or market value.BOE LTA 2026/002 and the Pub 800-10 information sheet.BOE · Prop 13 explanation ↗
Effect on the decisionIf market growth outruns the 2% assessed cap, an owner who keeps the protected base pays tax on a shrinking fraction of value. A sale resets that base for the buyer, not for this household.This is the mechanical reason the live-versus-rent gap widens over time.BOE · how property is assessed ↗
Effect of a reassessment eventA transfer that does not qualify for relief resets the base to market value, which can multiply the tax bill in one year.Prop 19 relief has occupancy, timing and filing conditions.California BOE · fact sheet ↗

The observation that most affects the ranking

In the invented example on the comparison page, the rental strategy is cash-negative before income tax at the assumed growth rate. The property tax line alone, computed on a reassessed market value, is larger than the modeled operating margin. That is not a quirk of the arithmetic; it is what full reassessment does to a long-held California property at today’s price-to-rent ratio.

The table below finds the gross monthly rent the rental would need just to cover operating costs, before financing and income tax, expressed in today’s money so it can be compared with an actual rent roll. If the achievable rent is below that line, renting loses to selling for reasons that have nothing to do with investment returns.
