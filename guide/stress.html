Black-swan sale-proceeds path: phase into stocks

Question: if a fictional heir sells at transfer, does holding net proceeds in a short U.S. Treasury ladder for 24 or 36 months, then moving into stocks over 12 months, reduce the damage from an early crash compared with investing immediately? Choose a waiting period and a flat annual Treasury-return proxy. The model runs the choice through all five paths; the early and late crash comparisons are highlighted.

Reinvestment and duration risk belong beside the headline. Treasury bills mature in 4–52 weeks (TreasuryDirect bills ↗); Treasury notes include 2-year securities (TreasuryDirect notes ↗). A ladder therefore returns principal at different dates, and maturing proceeds must be reinvested at then-available yields. The 1%, 3% and 5% rates here are invented flat sensitivities, not current yields or forecasts; the model does not simulate individual maturities, taxes, fees, changing rates, market-price losses on early sale, or inflation surprises. A fixed-income position can lose purchasing power, and its market value can fluctuate if sold before maturity (TreasuryDirect pricing ↗). Treasury interest is federally taxable and generally exempt from state and local income taxes (TreasuryDirect tax treatment ↗); obtain tax advice for actual holdings.

The phase-in is an explicit simplifying assumption: after the selected wait, half of the portfolio earns that year’s scenario stock return and half earns the Treasury proxy for one year; it is fully in the stock proxy the following year. This is annual arithmetic, not a quarterly trading schedule. No probabilities are assigned. The stress path is not a historical replay of 1929 or 2008.

03 / entry risk

Entry risk after a sale: invest now or phase in?

An entry-risk test, not a forecast. Phasing changes the price paid for the proceeds. It does not predict when a crash starts. The table compares starting at a market peak with waiting, using two crash shapes from the official record and a no-crash control.

A second view, on observed rates. The section above uses synthetic Treasury rates. This one uses the observed 3-month and 2-year Treasury yields on 13 Nov 2026 and the official 1929 and 2008 declines. The two are different tests and are not directly comparable.

Two official declines anchor the crash shapes. The Federal Reserve reports an 89 percent decline in stock prices from the 1929 peak to the trough (Fed 1929 ↗). It also reports that the S&P 500 fell 57 percent from its October 2007 peak to its March 2009 trough (Fed 2008 ↗). The 1929 template takes 34 months to reach its low and the 2008 template takes 17. Between peak and trough the path is a smooth geometric decline, not the actual index history. After the low, both shapes and the no-crash control grow at 4% or 8% a year. Those growth rates are assumptions, shown as a range.

The proceeds are per $100,000 and invented. Parked money earns the observed 3-month Treasury rate of 4.32%. Stocks are bought in 12 equal monthly tranches when the phasing begins. The mixed path puts one-third in at once and holds the rest for 24 months, then phases it in. The pure choices bracket it.

Read the 2008 park-24 row carefully. The template recovers from its trough at a constant rate, so every tranche bought after the trough grows exactly like the no-crash path. For that reason the 2008 park-24 values equal the no-crash park-24 values. The test shows the crash only through tranches bought before the trough. It does not model a recovery that stays below its earlier trend, which would change the result and is not included.

What the test does not show: a fast, V-shaped recovery such as 2008 favours investing all now. A slow decline and recovery favours waiting. Phasing is therefore a choice about which risk the household prefers to carry, not a forecast. Reviewers should confirm the templates against official index history before relying on exact figures.

03c / ladder and shape

Entry risk, second version: a recovery that returns to the prior peak, with the proceeds in a 3-month/2-year ladder

Why a second version of the test. The table above lets the index grow from its trough at the same 4% to 8% it grows at in the no-crash control. That is the reason its 2008 parked rows equal the control, and it flatters a peak entry: it never has to live with the fact that the 1929 high stood for a quarter of a century. The Federal Reserve states that the Dow peaked at 381.17 on 3 September 1929, closed at 41.22 on 8 July 1932 — 89 percent below the peak — and "did not return to its pre-crash heights until November 1954" (Fed 1929 ↗), which is 303 months after the peak. This version rebuilds the index so it reaches its prior peak at exactly that month for 1929, then grows at the same 4% to 8%. For 2008 it uses 66 months, labelled an assumption: no official page re-opened in this review states when the S&P 500 regained its October 2007 level, and 66 months is the figure the decision engine already carries.

The parked money is now a Treasury ladder rather than one flat rate. A three-month bill reprices every quarter, so what proceeds earn over a 24- or 36-month wait is a path, not a number. TreasuryDirect sells bills "for terms ranging from four weeks to 52 weeks" and notes for "a term of 2, 3, 5, 7, or 10 years" (bills ↗, notes ↗). The yields used are the observed par yields on 13 Nov 2026: 4.32% for three months and 4.88% for two years (Treasury ↗). Only a plan that waits before it buys can hold the note to maturity, so the note leg is priced only for the parking plans; a plan that needs money earlier would have to sell the note at market, which is not modeled here.

What this version says that the first one could not. Under a recovery that only reaches its starting level again after 25 years, the plan that invested everything at the peak spent every one of the 120 months in the window below the amount put in, and finished the decade at about a fifth of it. The parked plans never bought that top: parking 36 months was marked below the amount put in in no month at all, and parking 24 months and phasing into a market still falling spent 21 months under water before it turned. Both parked plans finished the decade above the peak entry in both crash shapes. In the 2008 shape the 24-month wait beat the 36-month one, because that low arrives at month 17 and a longer wait buys the climb instead of the bottom — the honest form of the finding, since the wait is priced against a shape nobody can know in advance. In the no-crash control the ranking turns on where equity growth sits against the parked yield: at 4% growth a two-year wait still ends slightly ahead, at 8% it ends about eighteen thousand behind per $100,000. That is the whole trade on one row of a table: phasing is insurance against the entry date, paid for with the growth of a good decade. No source reviewed here assigns a probability to either state.

Not modeled: individual bill maturities and auction discounts, the market value of a note sold before maturity, income tax on the interest or on the gain, transaction costs, the timing of the trust transfer, and any path between the peak and the low except a smooth geometric one. Reinvestment risk is the risk the SEC describes for money that has to be put to work again later — "faced with reinvesting the money at a lower, less attractive rate" (SEC Investor.gov ↗) — and the two middle columns of the parking table are that risk, priced.

04 / recorded history

The same test on recorded history, with the yields a crash actually pays

Why this section exists. The template tests above answer the entry question on two smooth shapes, with one observed yield applied flatly and a maturity ladder tried at three weights. This one replays the identical decision — park the net proceeds for 24 or 36 months in short Treasuries, then phase into stocks over 12 months — against recorded history: the actual December share-price index and the actual published 3-month and 2-year Treasury yields of each year. The reason it matters fits in one sentence: in a real crash the yield on parked money falls at the same time the index does, so waiting is not risk-free in either direction.

How this is built

Everything is per $100,000 of illustrative proceeds over ten years, the same invented amount the template test uses; no household figure is an input. Month 0 is the December before the entry year. The index between two year-ends is a geometric interpolation of the two December observations of the OECD U.S. share-price index, published by the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis; the December values for 1986, 1987, 2007, 2008 and 2009 were re-read from that official series on 16 Nov 2026. Parked cash earns, month by month, the published annual average of the H.15 constant-maturity yield for that calendar year, compounded at one-twelfth of the annual rate, because H.15 bill yields are quoted on an investment basis (3-month DGS3MO ↗, 2-year DGS2 ↗). A phase-in is twelve equal monthly tranches, sized from the balance the parking account holds when the window closes. The window is 1982 to 2016 for the entry year: the 3-month series begins on 1 September 1981, so nothing earlier can be replayed on recorded yields, and 2016 is the last entry year whose ten years end inside the price series.

Six readings a written policy should carry. (1) On the record, parking and phasing beat investing at once in 6 of 35 entry years, and every one of them sits at or just before a bear market (1999, 2000, 2001, 2006, 2007, 2008). (2) Its median price was −$42,860 per $100,000 over ten years, and the single worst entry year cost −$132,870. (3) It improved the worst point in the first three years in 11 of 35 entries, and the improvement was large only where a crash actually arrived: $42,570 at the 2008 entry. (4) The yield assumption matters more than the maturity choice: holding the 2008 parked cash at a flat 4.32% instead of the recorded 1.40%-then-0.15% path adds $17,610, about a fifth of the median cost of waiting, while moving the parking from 3-month bills to 2-year notes changed the ten-year outcome by no more than $11,620 across all entries and about $2,700 at the median. (5) A 36-month park costs roughly a year more of the same trade: −$61,620 at the median against −$42,860 for 24 months. (6) A 1929-style entry cannot be replayed on recorded yields here at all, so the template test above remains the only view of a deeper shape.

What this test does not contain

No transaction cost, no tax on the Treasury interest (it is federally taxable and generally exempt from California income tax, TreasuryDirect on bill terms ↗), no tax timing on the tranches, no inflation-linked securities, no bonds outside the two maturities named, and no allowance for changing one’s mind mid-parking. Sampling at December understates within-year swings: the same series shows 1987 ending 5.1% below 1986 although it fell 26.8% between its August 1987 monthly value and its December value. Nothing here is a forecast and no probability is attached to any entry year. The securities that make a real ladder behave differently from this arithmetic are described by the Bureau of the Fiscal Service: bills, 4 to 52 weeks ↗ and notes, 2 to 10 years ↗. The written policy these readings belong to is on What decides it; the adjustable version of the template test is on What-if controls.

Audit every year

Expand a path to inspect all 50 annual rates and outputs. The model caps annual factor growth at 2% and uses the lesser of market value and factored base as a simplified decline-in-value proxy; actual lien dates, assessment appeals, new construction and supplemental bills are not modeled.
