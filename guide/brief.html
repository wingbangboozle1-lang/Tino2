What actually decides it.

A decision-boundary view: which inputs flip the ranking, which ones a report can stop arguing about, and what would have to be true for a holding strategy to win.

Private facts withheldOfficial sources linkedExamples are synthetic
A decision brief, not a recommendation. Everything below is arithmetic on invented values, produced by the same model as the comparison page. Its purpose is to show which unknown facts would change the answer, so the reader knows where to spend effort. It is not advice about any real household, and it does not rank real properties.

The last row is the finding worth reading twice: the portfolio rate that makes selling lead is low and stable across home-price growth, because the holding strategies are carrying a large reassessed tax bill plus upkeep. Arguing about 3% versus 5% home appreciation changes far less than establishing whether a step-up or relief applies.

Decision boundary: which strategy leads

The grid below runs every combination of home-price growth and net portfolio return the comparison page exposes, for a 24-year hold after a 2045 transfer. Each cell shows the leading strategy for that pair of assumptions. The point is the shape, not any single cell.

S = sell and reinvest, R = rent then sell, L = live then sell. Every cell is a separate run of the model. No estate tax, financing, depreciation, recapture or value of occupying the home is modeled, so the boundary is a teaching illustration rather than a boundary for a real household.

What would have to be true for a holding strategy to lead

For renting to leadFor living there to leadOfficial context
The net portfolio return must be below about 3% at the assumed growth rate. In the observed inflation record, a 3% nominal return on a diversified portfolio would be a weak outcome over 24 years, but it is not impossible.Living must be valued at more than its modeled cash cost, and family-home relief must actually apply, otherwise the tax gap widens each year.SEC · diversification ↗
