Depreciation, recapture and the home-sale exclusion.

The rental tax the comparison model leaves out, priced from the IRS rules on the same synthetic house, with the two worked examples the IRS publishes as a check on the arithmetic.

Private facts withheldOfficial sources linkedSynthetic examples only
Why this page exists. The comparison model on this site deliberately omits depreciation and recapture, because both depend on facts no public page can know: the split between land and building, the date the property was placed in service, and the taxpayer’s own passive-activity position. That omission is not neutral — it flatters the renting paths, which get the deduction and not the bill. This page prices both, on the same synthetic house the comparison page uses, from the rules the IRS publishes. It does not change any figure elsewhere on the site, and it is not wired into the comparison.

The rules, as the official text states them

RuleWhat the official source saysOfficial source
Recovery periodResidential rental property (buildings or structures) and structural components such as furnaces, waterpipes, venting, etc. takes a 27.5-year recovery period under the general depreciation system, against 30 years under the alternative system.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
Method and conventionYou must use the straight line method and a mid-month convention for residential rental property. In the first year that you claim depreciation for residential rental property, you can claim depreciation only for the number of months the property is in use.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
What the mid-month convention doesA mid-month convention is used for all residential rental property and nonresidential real property. Under this convention, you treat all property placed in service, or disposed of, during any month as placed in service, or disposed of, at the midpoint of that month.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
The percentage tablesAppendix A of Publication 946 carries them: Table A-6 is for residential rental property using the mid-month convention and straight line depreciation over 27.5 years, and lists the percentages for years 1 through 29 by month placed in service.IRS · Publication 946, How To Depreciate Property (2025) ↗
Land is never depreciatedYou can’t depreciate the cost of land because land generally doesn’t wear out, become obsolete, or get used up. The costs of clearing, grading, planting and landscaping are usually part of the cost of land.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
Splitting land from buildingYou must divide the cost between the land and the buildings to figure the basis for depreciation of the buildings. The part allocated to each is the ratio of its fair market value to the value of the whole property; where you are not certain of those values, you may divide the cost based on their assessed values for real estate tax purposes.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
When depreciation startsYou begin to depreciate your rental property when you place it in service for the production of income. You place property in service in a rental activity when it is ready and available for a specific use in that activity, even if you are not using it.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
Moving from living there to letting itIf you used the property for personal purposes before changing it to rental use, its basis for depreciation is the lesser of its adjusted basis or its fair market value when you change it to rental use.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
Depreciation comes back on the saleDepreciation reduces your basis for figuring gain or loss on a later sale or exchange, and you must stop depreciating when the total of your yearly deductions equals your cost or other basis, including depreciation you were allowed to claim even if you did not claim it.IRS · Publication 527, Residential Rental Property (2025) ↗
Recapture rateThe portion of any unrecaptured section 1250 gain from selling section 1250 real property is taxed at a maximum 25% rate. That sits above the 0%, 15% and 20% rates that apply to most net capital gain.IRS · Topic 409 capital gains ↗
Where it is worked outSchedule D, line 19, is completed using the Unrecaptured Section 1250 Gain Worksheet where section 1250 property — generally, real property that you depreciated — held more than one year was sold or disposed of.IRS · Instructions for Schedule D (Form 1040) (2025) ↗
Where it is reportedThe sale of depreciable business or rental property is reported on Form 4797, whose net long-term gain then flows to Schedule D.IRS · Instructions for Form 4797 (2025) ↗
Home-sale exclusion, the amountYou may exclude the first $250,000 of gain from the sale of your home from your income and avoid paying taxes on it. The exclusion is increased to $500,000 for a married couple filing jointly.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Home-sale exclusion, ownershipIf you owned the home for at least 24 months (2 years) out of the last 5 years leading up to the date of sale, you meet the ownership requirement. For a married couple filing jointly, only one spouse has to meet it.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Home-sale exclusion, residenceIf you owned the home and used it as your residence for at least 24 months of the previous 5 years, you meet the residence requirement. The 24 months can fall anywhere within the 5-year period and need not be a single block of time; unlike the ownership test, each spouse must meet it individually.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
One main home at a timeThe exclusion applies only to the sale of a home that is your principal residence, and an individual has only one main home at a time. Where someone owns or lives in more than one home, a facts-and-circumstances test decides which one it is.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Look-backYou may take the exclusion only once during a 2-year period.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Depreciation is never excludedYou cannot exclude the part of your gain equal to any depreciation allowed or allowable as a deduction for periods after May 6, 1997. It is unrecaptured section 1250 gain and is taxed at its own rate.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Nonqualified useGain from the sale or exchange of your main home isn’t excludable from income if it is allocable to periods of nonqualified use. Nonqualified use means any period after 2008 when neither you nor your spouse (or former spouse) used the property as your main home, with certain exceptions.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
The exception that saves the ordinary caseA period of nonqualified use does not include any portion of the 5-year period ending on the date of the sale after the last date the property was used as a main home. Renting the house out after moving away therefore does not, by itself, create nonqualified use.IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Passive-activity limitsA rental loss is not automatically usable in the year it arises. The passive activity and at-risk rules decide when it can be deducted. This page shows the suspended amount rather than crediting it.IRS · Publication 925, Passive Activity and At-Risk Rules ↗

Quoted from the IRS publications named in each row, read 17 November 2026. Nothing here is a household figure, and nothing is inferred: where a rule depends on a fact this site does not hold, the page says so.

Check the arithmetic against the examples the IRS prints

Publication 523 contains two fully worked examples. Their facts and every number below are the IRS’s own; this site runs them through the same worksheet code it uses for the synthetic example further down, so the page and the publication agree before any scenario is shown.

Example and its factsTotal gainDepreciation recaptured at 25%Gain allocated to nonqualified useGain excluded under §121Capital gain left to taxOfficial source
Finley — rented first, then lived there (the IRS example)
Bought 1 January 2020 for $400,000 and rented it for two years, claiming $20,000 of depreciation. Converted to a main home on 1 January 2022, moved out on 1 January 2024, sold on 1 January 2025 for $700,000.		$320,000$20,000$120,000$180,000$0IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
Taylor — lived there first, then rented it (the IRS example)
Bought 1 January 2020 for $400,000 as a main home, moved out on 1 January 2023 and rented it immediately. Sold on 1 December 2024 for $600,000, having deducted $27,000 of depreciation.		$227,000$27,000$0$200,000$0IRS · Publication 523, Selling Your Home (2025) ↗
The one sentence worth reading twice. Finley rented first and lived there later: two of five years were nonqualified use, so 40% of the gain was carved out of the exclusion and only $180,000 of a $320,000 gain was excluded. Taylor lived there first and rented later: the rental period fell after the last qualified use, so nothing was carved out and the whole of the remaining gain was excluded — but the depreciation still came back at 25%. Same house value, same two years of renting, different order, and the exclusion differs by $160,000.

Two traps this table makes visible. First, renting for long enough after moving out pushes the two years of residence outside the five-year window before the sale, and the exclusion is lost entirely — the two years of living there earn nothing. Second, renting first and moving back in keeps the exclusion but hands most of the gain to the nonqualified-use allocation, so on a long hold the $250,000 or $500,000 exclusion is a rounding error against the gain.

Imputed rent: crediting the living path

A household that lives in the house pays no rent, and that saving is real money that never appears in a cash-flow table. It is a preference, not a cash flow, so this site credits it only when asked. The What-if controls expose the same credit as an input on the comparison itself; this page keeps a second input because it also prints the break-even, which is the number that ends the argument.
