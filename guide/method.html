03 / method

How the comparison is made

  1. Same endpoint. Every option is valued at the same date, after the sale costs that would be paid to turn the house into cash.
  2. Same reinvestment. Cash flows from holding are reinvested at the same net return as the sale proceeds, so neither option gets a hidden advantage.
  3. Taxes actually applicable. Selling at inheritance pays no gain tax because basis resets. Renting pays income tax on positive net operating income after depreciation, and recapture at exit. Living in gets the home-sale exclusion and the Proposition 19 relief.
  4. Separate properties. Property B is never folded into the comparison except for the third unit, which only becomes rentable if the household lives in A.
  5. What is left out. Estate tax, financing, the value of living in the house, and transaction costs that have not been quoted.
04 / stress method

How the stress tests work

Two different methods are used because they answer different questions.

Named scenarios impose a specific historical sequence, such as the 1970s inflation path or the 2007-2012 financial crisis, at the start of the holding period. They show what happens if a known bad decade arrives at the wrong moment.

Resampled histories take the actual joint record of annual California inflation and Santa Clara County house-price change from 1976 to 2025 and resample it in five-year blocks, 2,000 times, keeping some of the serial correlation in the data. They answer a different question: across the range of conditions the record actually contains, how often does each option win, and how wide is the spread?

Neither method predicts the future. A model that assumed constant growth would systematically understate the risk of holding a single house, which is why this page exists.
