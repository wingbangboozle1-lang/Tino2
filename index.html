Executive summary

Make the decision after the critical facts are known.

Property A is the anticipated Cupertino trust inheritance; Property B is an already-owned San Francisco three-unit property of the same heir, not part of the decedent’s estate. Two B units rent for a month combined and a third is available to the owner without displacing those rents (owner-stated premise, not independently verified). Every household figure on this site is rounded to the nearest $10 and every identifier is withheld.

What the numbers say today. A is assessed at and taxed about a year (2025); on the model’s growth assumptions the transfer resets that base sharply — the first post-transfer bill lands near a year if the household lives there and a year if it is rented out. Across the stress tests no single path wins everywhere: the baseline favors selling and reinvesting, 1929- and 2008-style crash entries favor selling and phasing in over 24–36 months, and 1970s-style stagflation favors living in it. The path with the smallest worst-case regret is live two years, then sell and phase.

Recommendation, conditional: plan on selling A and phasing the net proceeds into stocks — unless the Priority 1 facts (trust powers, basis step-up, Proposition 19 eligibility, estate size) come back different, or the household values living there. Keep B: its rents continue under every path. The eight facts that can change this are listed here.

03 / the finding

The finding, in one paragraph

On the household’s own rounded inputs, selling the inherited residence and reinvesting the net proceeds leads in the baseline runs once the portfolio earns a modest net return. The reason is structural rather than a matter of market timing: a rented rather than occupied property carries a property tax bill computed on a reassessed market value, plus upkeep that rises with price, and those lines exceed the modeled rent (HUD benchmark a month against a break-even of a month at the 25-year horizon). The live path avoids part of that tax through the family-home allowance but gives up the rent; it wins only under 1970s-style stagflation. What actually changes the answer is not the return assumption — it is whether a basis step-up applies, whether family-home relief is available, whether the trust permits a sale at all, and whether house growth exceeds about 5.8% a year (the published break-even).

03b / reinvestment

Reinvestment risk, beside the headline

Proceeds that wait before they are invested earn the parking yield, and their value at the end depends on that yield. The observed U.S. Treasury par yields on 13 Nov 2026 were 4.32% for 3 months and 4.88% for 2 years. The 2.50% row is a stress assumption for falling short rates, not an observation. Only the parking yield changes between rows. Values are per $100,000 of proceeds, after 10 years, with post-trough growth between 4% and 8% a year.
