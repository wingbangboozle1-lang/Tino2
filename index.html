A clearer way to plan

A source-linked guide to comparing a possible Cupertino inheritance with an already-owned San Francisco property, without publishing private records.

Private facts withheldOfficial sources linkedExamples are synthetic
Executive summary

Make the decision after the critical facts are known.

Property A is the anticipated Cupertino trust inheritance. Property B is an already-owned San Francisco three-unit property, not automatically part of the decedent’s estate. The planning premise is that two units are rented and a third is available for the owner; occupying that unit does not displace the other two rents. This premise is supplied, not independently verified. B’s value and continuing rent must be modeled separately.

Working direction: compare selling A and investing the net proceeds with renting it, while keeping B; consider living in A if the household values that residence and meets all conditions for reassessment relief. Do not treat any example as the best plan for a real household.

03 / the finding

The finding, in one paragraph

On every set of invented assumptions this site tests, selling the inherited residence and reinvesting the net proceeds leads once the portfolio earns a modest net return, and it leads by a wide margin in the middle case. The reason is structural rather than a matter of market timing: a property that is rented rather than occupied carries a property tax bill computed on a reassessed market value, plus upkeep that rises with price, and on this model those two lines exceed the rent. The live strategy avoids part of the tax but gives up the rent, so it trails both. What actually changes the answer is not the return assumption — it is whether a basis step-up applies, whether family-home relief is available, and whether the trust permits a sale at all.
