Inherit the Cupertino house:
live in it, rent it, or sell?

One house (Property A, Cupertino) is expected to pass by trust between 2040 and 2050. The heir already owns and can live in a three-unit building in San Francisco (Property B). This page gives the answer on the numbers, the reasons, and what is still unknown. Every table row links to an official source or is marked as not confirmed.

The San Francisco property changes the question

The heir does not need a second home

Living in A is a choice, not a need

The heir can live in Property B's third unit and keep both rents (about $7,150 a month). Moving into A gives up everything A could earn if sold, which is why it finishes last on money.

Household comparison →

One principal residence

The Proposition 19 relief needs the heir to move into A within one year and stay. Staying in San Francisco and claiming it is not possible; renting A out gets no relief at all.

Both houses sit in one region

Keeping A doubles the household's exposure to one earthquake, one regional downturn and one state's property-tax law. Selling A moves that part into other assets.

Property tax is the cost that changes. Today the house is taxed on an assessed value of about $550,540; at the inheritance it is reassessed to market value, less the Proposition 19 amount ($1,044,586 until February 2027, adjusted every two years) only if the heir lives there. Insurance ($1,500 a year) and water ($125 a month) are the household's figures grown 3% a year. No official premium for the home exists. The state regulator's 2026 survey of a made-up Cupertino home runs from about $1,380 to $2,690 a year in the middle of the list, depending on what the house would cost to rebuild; no premium in that range changes the best plan (operating costs).
