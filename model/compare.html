Why pages differ

Nine sessions were given the same brief at the same time, and several of them published pages in this repository. The pages agree on what to do and disagree on the dollar amounts, because they assume different growth rates, tax rates and returns. This page sets the assumptions side by side and reports a scan of everything the repository publishes.

What the differences do

  • Growth rate. At 3% a year Property A is worth about $5,435,870 in 2045; at 3.5%, $5,959,750; at 4.29%, $6,886,080. Every later figure scales with this.
  • Tax rate. Dividing a bill by the taxed value counts the fixed parcel charges as if they grew with the house’s value. After a revaluation to $6,886,080 that overstates the bill: at 1.34% the tax would be $92,270, against $81,760 with the published rate plus fixed charges.
  • Proposition 19 amount. It changes every second year by one year’s change in the state house-price index. Holding it flat understates it; these pages apply the scenario’s index change at each adjustment.
  • Returns. A single 5% or 6% return and a range from 4.65% to 11.40% give very different end balances for “sell and invest”, though selling comes out highest at each of them in the middle house-price case.

The calculator accepts any of these assumptions, so each set of pages can be reproduced and compared on the same footing.

What this means

  • The repository is public. ZIP codes narrow each property to a small area, and an exact assessed value or tax bill can be matched against public assessor records.
  • Earlier commits contain the brief’s figures before rounding. Rounding the current files does not remove them from the history.
  • These model pages use only the city names and figures rounded to $10 that were already published, and add no ZIP code, neighbourhood, year-by-year bill history or contact detail.
  • Only the owner can close the gap: make the repository private, or rewrite its history and republish.
