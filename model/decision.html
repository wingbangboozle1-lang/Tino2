Decision

Three things can be done with Property A after it is inherited. This page compares them in money, shows what would have to be true for the answer to change, and then looks at Property B.

The three choices

Sell and invest

Sell within months of the death at the appraised value. No gain is taxed, because the basis is the value at death. Invest what is left after selling costs. The household stays in Property B.

Rent it out

Keep the house and let it. It is re-taxed on its full market value. Rent is taxed after expenses and depreciation; the depreciation is taxed back when the house is sold. The household stays in Property B.

Live in it

Move in within one year and claim the parent-to-child exclusion, which keeps part of the old tax base. The unit left empty in Property B is rented. Up to $500,000 of later gain is tax-free on sale.

All three are followed for the same number of years, and then everything is sold and taxed, so that a house and an investment account can be compared like for like. Property B’s own rent and costs are the same in all three and are left out.

What the money figures leave out

  • What the family wants. A larger home, a school district, a place with memories: none has a price here. The figure to weigh them against is the yearly price of living in Property A, above.
  • Work and risk of being a landlord for a single house: vacancies, repairs, one tenant.
  • Concentration. Keeping both homes leaves most of the family’s wealth in Bay Area housing. The house-price index for both areas fell by about a quarter after 2006.
  • Market risk the other way. Invested money can also fall sharply in a single year; the returns used are long-run averages.
  • A later step-up. Assets held until the heirs’ own deaths get a new basis under today’s law, whether they are houses or shares, so this does not favour one choice.
